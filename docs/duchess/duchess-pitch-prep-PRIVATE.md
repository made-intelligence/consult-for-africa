## 0b. ISO 31000, in enough depth to hold a conversation

Their terms of reference name it, so a board member may well test you on it.

**The one fact that matters most: ISO 31000 is a guideline, not a certifiable standard.** You cannot be certified or accredited to it, and there is no audit that confers it. Anyone offering to certify a hospital against ISO 31000 is selling something ISO itself does not recognise. Saying this plainly is worth a great deal of credibility in the room, and it also protects them from a vendor who will offer exactly that.

**The current edition is ISO 31000:2018**, published February 2018, replacing the 2009 version. The revision tightened the language and, most importantly, moved **top management accountability to the centre of the framework**. That is the hook into their governance: the Audit and Risk Sub-committee has to own the tolerance line. We design it, they own it.

**Its definition of risk is "the effect of uncertainty on objectives."** Deliberately neutral, so risk includes upside as well as threat. That definition is what allows a board to hold opportunity and exposure on one register, and it is the reason an enterprise framework can cover financial and strategic risk without contorting a clinical safety model to fit.

**It has three parts and you should be able to name them.**

- **Principles.** What good looks like. Risk management should be integrated into the organisation rather than bolted on, structured and comprehensive, customised to the context, inclusive of the people who hold the risk, dynamic as the context changes, built on the best available information, alive to human and cultural factors, and continually improved. All of it in service of one thing, the creation and protection of value.
- **Framework.** The governance conditions that let it work. Leadership and commitment sits at the centre, surrounded by integration, design, implementation, evaluation and improvement. This is the part most hospitals skip, and it is why their register goes stale.
- **Process.** What actually runs. Communication and consultation, then scope, context and criteria, then risk assessment, which is identification, analysis and evaluation, then treatment, then monitoring and review, then recording and reporting.

**Companion standards worth naming if pressed.** **IEC 31010** is the toolbox of risk assessment techniques, which is where failure mode and effects analysis, bow tie analysis, scenario analysis and the rest actually live. **ISO Guide 73** is the vocabulary. If someone asks which technique we will use, the honest answer is that 31010 offers dozens and the diagnostic decides which fit, rather than picking one now to sound decisive.

**Treatment options, in their language.** Avoid the activity. Take or increase the risk to pursue an opportunity. Remove the source. Change the likelihood. Change the consequence. Share it, through contract or insurance. Or retain it by informed decision, which is the one boards do by accident and should be doing deliberately.

**One more frame worth having, though it is not ISO.** The **three lines of defence**: the first line owns and manages risk day to day, the second sets policy and monitors, the third gives independent assurance. In a hundred bed private hospital the third line is almost always the gap, because there is rarely an internal audit function. That is a finding you can predict before you walk in, and it is a natural role for their Audit and Risk Sub-committee to commission rather than for us to occupy.

## 4. The line that wins the room

This is the thing to say, and it corrects our own proposal while making us look like the only people in the process who have actually run a hospital.

> "If this engagement works, your incident reports will go up in the first year, not down. That is not a failure. A hospital that reports more incidents is a hospital where people have stopped being afraid to report. The number that should fall is harm, and severity, and repeat events of the same type. Any consultant who promises you fewer incident reports in year one is promising you a quieter system, not a safer one, and those are very different things."

Say it slowly. Then the follow through:

> "So we will not measure ourselves on report volume. We will measure on reporting rate against activity, on time from event to report, on the proportion of events with a completed root cause analysis, on repeat events of the same type, and on how many risks on the register actually moved. Those are the indicators we will build into your dashboard."

That single exchange does four things at once. It demonstrates domain command that no generalist firm can fake. It pre-frames the key risk indicator conversation, which is one of their four stated objectives. It protects us from being judged later on a metric that would make good work look like failure. And it quietly removes the unsourced claim from our March document without ever drawing attention to it.

## 5. The pitch spine

Forty minutes of content at most, and leave a third of the time for them.

**Open, two minutes.** Thank them. Ask what has changed since March. Listen. Do not pitch yet.

**The problem as we see it, four minutes.** Every hospital that commissions this work gets a risk register. Very few have a live one twelve months later. The framework is not the hard part. Any competent firm in this process can write you a policy suite that reads well. The hard part is whether the system is still running after the consultant leaves, and that is decided by three things: whether it was built on evidence your own people produced, whether the reporting line to the board actually functions, and whether somebody inside this hospital owns it.

**The counter-intuitive truth, two minutes.** Section 4. This is your credibility moment.

**Method, eighteen minutes. This is the forty per cent.** Four pillars, in this order.

1. **Evidence, not assumption.** Four independent streams, chosen so that no two fail the same way: structured interviews across departments, an anonymous staff safety culture survey, a patient and caregiver experience survey, and documentary analysis of what the hospital already produces. When streams disagree, the disagreement is the finding. On one recent hospital diagnostic the interview evidence alone would have led us to upgrade the revenue cycle, and the documents forced it back down. We would have been wrong without the fourth stream.

2. **The ten case trace.** The differentiator, and nobody else will offer it. Ten real patient journeys, chosen on the morning with their clinical lead present so nothing can be curated, each followed through every link in the chain: presentation, decision to treat, consent, pre-operative assessment, the surgical safety checklist, antibiotic timing, sterilisation load records and instrument traceability, the observation record, escalation and the deteriorating patient, handover, discharge, follow up attendance, the incident log and any complaint. Tracing ten completely beats sampling a hundred partially, because every gap is specific rather than theoretical. You are not telling a board that documentation is inconsistent. You are telling them that in this case, on this date, the sterilisation load record cannot be linked to the patient, so if that load were recalled they could not identify who it touched.

3. **A board line that works.** Their own terms of reference send progress to the Board Audit and Risk Sub-committee. Most consultants have never sat on the receiving end of one of those reports. You have. You are an independent non-executive director of a Lagos specialist hospital and you built its board and committee instruments: the risk summary, the exception report, the escalation thresholds, the registers, the dashboard and the action log. You know the difference between a risk report a board reads and one a board files.

4. **An owner inside the hospital.** Our proposal already commits to identifying and coaching an internal Clinical Governance Champion. Strengthen it in the room: we assess, train and formally certify that person, with a competency check, and we design the role brief so it survives the individual leaving. **Track record, eight minutes. This is the thirty per cent.** Section 6.

**The team, four minutes. This is the twenty per cent.** Named people, not roles. See section 9, it is a decision you must take before you walk in.

**Investment, two minutes. This is the ten per cent.** Say the number without apology, explain the resourcing behind it, move on. Do not defend it. Do not discount in the room.
