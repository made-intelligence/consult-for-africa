How to use this book

This is the single reference for how Consult for Africa grows. When a plan, a pitch or a campaign contradicts it, the book wins until the book is changed, and when the book is wrong, change the book.

Who it is for

Everyone who brings work into the firm or keeps it here: the partners and directors who lead engagements, the business development lead, the marketing team, the Office of the Founding Partner who keep the record, and every consultant on a client site, because most of our next work is noticed by the person already in the building. It assumes no prior context.

How it relates to the other documents

The Marketing Handbook holds the brands, the voice and the anonymised case study library. The Marketing Operating Brief sets the agency's service levels and KPIs. The Growth Pack is the business development lead's briefing on each client and the live pipeline. This book sits above all three and explains the system they belong to, so it points to them rather than repeating them.

How it is built

Five parts and 28 modules. Each module opens with what you will be able to do after reading it, teaches one idea, shows what is done and what is pending, and ends with questions to check yourself. Read Parts I and II in full. Read the modules in Part III and IV that match your work. Keep Part V open.

The one idea

Clients buy advice from people they trust, and trust reaches them through other people. The firm grows when more people can vouch for us, when they can find and understand us once they have heard our name, and when each engagement leads to the next one.

Today nearly all of that trust runs through one person, Dr Debo Odulana, and his relationships from Doctoora, Evercare, Cedarcrest and the boards he sits on. That is why our win rate is high and our pipeline is thin and unforecastable. The purpose of this book is to turn one man's network into a firm's reputation without losing what makes the network work.

When you are unsure what to prioritise, ask which choice puts us in front of more people who could vouch for us, or turns more of today's clients into tomorrow's.

Medical travel is a domestic opportunity

Roughly $650 to $700 million a year leaves the country for treatment abroad4, and a Health Federation of Nigeria estimate puts total Nigerian health spending at $34 billion in 20266. Every "bring it home" thesis we have worked on (Clearview's fertility destination, Osteon's revision arthroplasty, the Lyfe Place medipark) rests on the same observation, which is that local care is often already better value and is not believed. That is a trust problem, and trust problems are what we sell against.

Quality starts low and moves when measured

In one SafeCare programme in Kaduna, 17 of 25 private facilities started at the lowest of five levels, and on reassessment 19 of them had moved up one or two levels8. The sample is small and the lesson is general: most private facilities have never been measured, and once they are, they improve. That is the case for the audit, and the case for accreditation readiness as an offer.

Where the facilities are

Lagos had 3,670 registered health facilities when HEFAMAA last published a count in 202011, and the agency continues to seal facilities that fail inspection. A current national count of private hospitals is not reliably published, so build target lists from named facilities rather than from a market-size number.

Numbers we never cite

"91.47% out of pocket", any "Africa healthcare market worth $259 billion by 2030" figure (no traceable source), "70 plus accredited HMOs" (no NHIA list behind it), "3.9 doctors per 10,000" until we have it from the source, and any 2022 or earlier naira price used as a 2026 anchor. Module 3

How organisations buy advice

After this module you can explain, with evidence, why most of the people who will hire us are not looking today, why referrals fail when the referred firm is hard to understand, and what that means for how we spend our time.

Professional services are bought differently from products. The buyer cannot inspect the work before paying for it, so they buy the firm's reputation and the person in front of them. The research on how that happens is consistent, and it explains both our strengths and our gap.

Seven findings that shape this book

FindingThe evidenceWhat we do about it
Most buyers are not in the marketThe Ehrenberg-Bass Institute estimates that only about 5% of business buyers are in market for a given category at any time17.A hospital owner changes adviser rarely. We have to be remembered in the years between purchases, so most of our effort goes into being known, not into chasing.

Referrals come from non-clients too81.5% of professional services firms had received a referral from someone who had never been a client, and nearly half of those referrals were based on the firm's visible expertise16.Publishing what we know creates referrers we have never met.

High-growth firms behave differently

Hinge's long-running study of professional services firms defines high growth as 20% a year or more for three years. In its consulting sample, high-growth firms were 82% more likely to research their target market regularly and 52% more likely to spend 15% or more of revenue on marketing14. The 2025 edition, covering 770 firms, found high-growth firms growing four times faster and 30% more profitable than their peers15. The two habits that separate them are the two we have least of: systematic research on our own market, and a deliberate budget for being known.

Keeping a client is cheaper than finding one

The figure usually cited, from work by Bain's Fred Reichheld reported in Harvard Business Review, is that acquiring a new customer costs five to twenty-five times as much as keeping an existing one, and that a five-point rise in retention lifts profit by 25 to 95%24. The figures come from other industries and should be quoted as such, but our own record points the same way: the Medbury division, the Haven growth mandate and the House of Refuge retainer all began as smaller pieces of work for the same client.

What this means in one paragraph

Our network-led model already does the hard part, which is trust. What it cannot do is reach the 95% who are not yet looking, explain us to a stranger who has just been given our name, or remember to ask the client who would happily refer us. Those three gaps are what Parts II and III are built to close.

The buying group

The person who calls us is rarely the only person who decides. In a doctor-owned hospital the spouse, a sibling on the board and the accountant all have a view, and in a group the finance director and the business unit heads do. Assume every document we send will be forwarded to someone we have not met, and write it so that it works without us in the room. A proposal that only makes sense with Debo explaining it will lose to one that explains itself.

Profiles of the people we sell to most

The founder-physician, 55, Lagos. Built a forty-bed hospital over twenty years. Revenue flat since 2023, costs not. Trusts doctors more than consultants, and trusts people who have run a hospital more than either. Needs: someone who understands without being told, a finding he did not expect, and discretion.

The second-generation director, 38. Returned from abroad to a family hospital. Wants governance and a plan, and needs an outside voice the older generation will accept. Needs: a structure that settles who decides, and a document that can be shown to the family.

The group chief executive. Runs several business units with strong leads she will not override. Needs: options presented as decisions, respect for her managers' autonomy, and proposals split by who signs.

The investor or lender. Has capital and a thesis and no operator. Needs: an honest options note with ranges, what has to be true, and what kills the deal, plus someone who can run the asset afterwards.

The rules that keep it honest

  • Every record carries a source: Debo introduction, client referral, non-client referral, partner, inbound web, inbound WhatsApp, event, content, target account outreach, tender. Without it Module 7's headline number cannot be measured.
  • Every record carries a next action and a date. The Thursday sweep (Module 26) lists every opportunity with no logged action in fourteen days.
  • Forty-five days without a response closes the opportunity as lost with a reason, or moves it to nurture with a date to revisit. Closing is not failure; an honest pipeline is the point.
  • A draft proposal older than thirty days is sent, rewritten or closed. Six-month-old drafts make every forecast meaningless.
  • Inbound enquiries get a reply from a named person within one working hour. In the HBR audit of 2,241 firms the average response took 42 hours and 23% never responded21, so answering the same hour is the cheapest differentiator we have. The one website enquiry of the last month went unanswered for weeks because no inbox had an owner; that is a system gap, and the Office of the Founding Partner now owns it.

Who owns what

Debo owns price. The BD lead owns the shape of the deal: what is in scope, the stages, the timetable and the terms. Engagement leads own the delivery plan and the team. Nobody else discusses a fee with a client, and nobody discusses one before Debo has set the opening position.

How a proposal is built

  • Answer first. The opening page states what we recommend and what it will achieve for the client, in their numbers. RAIN Group's research on what sales winners do differently found buyers rank demonstrated value above everything else23.
  • Decisions, not recommendations. Present options so the board is choosing, not being told.
  • Deck for the principal, prose for the file. A busy principal gets a ten to fourteen slide landscape deck; the prose document is the leave-behind. Both must stand on their own, because buyers spend most of their decision time without us20.
  • Split by who signs. Where a group has strong business unit leads, send group-level and unit-level proposals to the people who own each decision.
  • No product names in the body. Describe what is done. Name products only in an appendix the client asked for.
  • An opening partner proposal carries no fees. When the relationship is a partnership, the first document sets out the model and principles; figures are discussed in a meeting.

How we price

Price has more leverage on profit than volume. In Marn and Rosiello's classic analysis, a 1% price improvement with no loss of volume lifted operating profit by 11.1%, against 3.3% for a 1% rise in volume25. Holding our price on the work we win matters more than winning one more engagement at a discount.

A proposal is sent with a date to discuss it, never into silence. After the meeting, follow up within one working day with a written summary of what was agreed and what remains open.
